Research the company described below to judge whether it, or someone who works there, would make a good design partner for the product described in the overview. We are not selling. We are looking for people who will try an early version and give honest feedback.

Cite a source for every claim. If you can't find evidence for something, say "No evidence found" rather than guessing.

## 1. What they do

One short paragraph: their product or service and who they serve.

## 2. Size and stage

Headcount, funding stage, and growth signals. Note whether the team is small or autonomous enough that individuals can try new tools without a procurement process.

## 3. Do they have the problem?

Evidence that this team deals with the problem from the overview today: job posts, blog posts, product pages, team structure, public comments by employees. Quote or link the evidence.

## 4. How they handle it today

Tools, processes, or workarounds they appear to use for this problem, including tools adjacent to ours.

## 5. Openness to new tools

Signs of early-adopter behavior: joining betas, posting about tools they've tried, building in public, a fast product changelog, an engineering or ops blog.

## 6. Who to talk to

The people closest to the problem day to day (hands-on practitioners and the person most likely to champion trying something new), not only executives. For each person, one line on why they're relevant. Prefer these people when writing the people list.

## 7. Red flags

Reasons this would be a poor fit: enterprise procurement, locked into an established competitor, the problem isn't present, or they're unlikely to have time for feedback.

## 8. Verdict

Strong fit, possible fit, or unlikely, with a two-sentence reason.
